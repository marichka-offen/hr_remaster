Colonial History Virginia British Army

British Regulars in Virginia

1677 — 1682

The formation, deployment and dissolution of Jeffreys' Regiment of Foot — one of the first British regular army units sent to colonial America.

By Lee Offen ~25 min read Primary Source Research

The Call to Arms

September–November 1676

In late September 1676, the ship Rebecca arrived in London with dispatches from the Vice Governor of Virginia, Sir William Berkeley, with word of a rebellion by the colonists. The Governor, Thomas Lord Culpepper, had been the governor for life since 1675 but had not yet even seen the colony.

Acting with uncharacteristic speed, by 1 October King Charles II had resolved to send a thousand men to quell the mutineers. For the time, this was a major expedition incurring considerable cost for a government always short of money. Within four more days, commissions for five foot companies — one each from existing standing regiments — were signed, and Jeffreys' Regiment of Foot came into being.

The companies were armed with the typical ratio of 2:1 musket to pike, with the possible exception of the Duke of York's Regiment which was musket-only. Unusually, the companies wore the uniforms of their parent regiments, and the regimental colors reflected these origins:

Warrant to Ralph Montague, Master of the King's Great Wardrobe. To prepare two colours for each of the five companies of the King's foot guards, now sending upon an expedition to Virginia, viz., for Captain Herbert Jeffreys' company, two colours, with a crowned lion passant upon the crown; for Captain Edward Picks' company two colours, with the Royal oak crowned; for Captain John Mutlowe's company two colours, the ground blue with a red cross in a white field; for Captain Charles Middleton's company two colours, white waved with lemon, equally mixed with the red cross quite through with J. D. Y. in cipher, in gold; and for Captain William Meole's company two colours, the ground green with a red cross in a white field.

The 1,500 "swinefeathers" — an archaic item consisting of a 6-to-7 foot pikestaff with steel points at both ends and a hook to support a musket — were likely taken to serve as field expedient chevaux de frise obstacles. They were included in an 1678 inventory at the Middle Plantation and doubtless never saw use.

Arrival in Virginia

January–May 1677

The first ship arriving at the ruins of Jamestown was the Bristol on 29 January 1677, carrying Sir John Berry and Captain Morris with seventy soldiers. The rest of the fleet arrived by 5 February. The ten-week crossing had taken a devastating toll:

In Captain Middleton's Company there was about 150 such men and officers all sick and that the like number of sick were in every other company.

Commissioners to the King, June 1677

The Regiment was forced to rely on colonists who had barely enough to take care of themselves in the wake of Bacon's Rebellion. By February, the Regiment was headquartered at the Middle Plantation — the future site of Williamsburg — with companies distributed to the Falls of the James (future Richmond), Nansemond County, and New Kent County.

On 29 May 1677, the Regiment was paraded for the ceremonial signing of peace with local tribes, at which were present the Queen of the Pamunkey, her son, the Queen of Weyanoke, the King of the Nottoways, and King of the Nansemonds.

An Open Question

A total of 375 soldiers returned to England out of Jeffreys' Regiment. Excluding the 100 who remained in service, that means 525 soldiers either died or chose to stay in Virginia as civilians. It is interesting that the Unity returned primarily with soldiers from the 1st and 2nd Guards. Could those who stayed have been predominantly from the Duke of York's Regiment and the Holland Regiment?

Colonel Jeffreys died in December 1678. Sir Henry Chicheley replaced him as Lieutenant Governor and Captain. By 1680, the Virginia Assembly asked that soldiers replace militia at four garrison houses on the Rappahannock, Potomac, James and Mattaponi Rivers — but 38 of those 40 soldiers were described as mutinous, likely due to lack of pay.

The end came in May 1682. The cutting of tobacco plants while still in the fields — a desperate effort to raise prices — occurred simultaneously with the disbandment. The soldiers, unsatisfied with terms, only contributed to the problem they could have helped quell:

They are therefore so far from being an assistance at the only moment they are wanted since their arrival, that their mutinous temper doubles our apprehension of evil events.

Secretary of Virginia to Sir Leoline Jenkins, 1682

The disbandment was complete by June 1682. Arms, partisans, halberds and drums were returned to store. Eight soldiers were retained as the Governor's Guard. James Archer, the engineer, appears in the record as late as 1690 as a Captain in the Virginia Militia — one of the few traceable threads of the regiment's legacy.

Conclusions

Given the absence of military action, it is difficult to assess the contribution of Jeffreys' Regiment. Their contribution may have been preemptive — perhaps their presence prevented colonial unrest and Indian incursions rather than stopping them in process. A few officers' names reoccur in positions of colonial responsibility, so they continued to influence events.

The Regiment was unique in retaining parent-unit colors and uniforms, and in the speed of its formation. It represents one of the first examples of sending British Regulars to colonies to assert Crown control over territories that had largely been ignored — a pattern that would define the next century of British colonial policy.

The record remains frustratingly incomplete. Large gaps in time exist for which no information about the units has been found. Any insights or sources that would help make a more complete record would be greatly appreciated.
