Hussite Wars, 1420–1434

Background

Kingdom of Bohemia

The Kingdom of Bohemia was predominantly Slavic in ethnicity, having been established by the Slavs who settled in what had been a Celtic region of Eastern Europe. In the 13th century, the presence of large silver deposits and the willingness of its rulers to encourage ethnic Germans to exploit and develop this wealth resulted in a large German minority in Bohemia. The Germans came to occupy positions of prominence in the trade guilds and churches in the urban areas like Prague, Kutná Hora (Kuttenberg), and Německý Brod (Deutschbrod). This influx of Germans, and their subsequent prominent role, created considerable resentment in the Slavic native population.

Jan Hus (1371–1415)

Reformer · Rector of Charles University · Martyr

Jan Hus, an ethnic Slav, graduated from Charles University (Univerzita Karlova) in Prague and in 1398 began lecturing there. He served as rector from 1402–03 and 1409–10 and was an extremely popular clergyman among the Slavs, both noble and peasant, preaching to all on the hills outside Prague. He became a proponent of the ideas of the English reformer and theologian John Wycliffe. Hus, like Wycliffe, denounced the immorality of clergy, the sale of indulgences, the accumulation of wealth by the clergy and the church, and saw the Bible as the final authority in ecclesiastical matters.

He was a proponent of the laity taking communion in both kinds — that is to say the bread and the wine — at a time when only the clergy received communion in both kinds. He translated the Bible into the language of the people and said "Women were made in the image of God and should fear no man," setting the stage for women to preach at Hussite services, participate in governing councils, and fight beside their men in battle. It was his teaching and writing about these radical ideas that brought the attention of the Pope to Jan Hus. Hus was commanded by one of (at the time) two Popes to appear before a General Council of the Church in November 1414 at Constance in Germany.

The Holy Roman Emperor, Sigismund, granted him a safe conduct to travel from Prague to Constance and back. Upon his arrival at Constance, Hus was immediately imprisoned and the Council convened to try him for heresy. A group of 452 nobles from the Kingdom of Bohemia and Poland-Lithuania attempted to intercede on Hus's behalf, but to no avail.

The Goose & The Swan

Hus was burned at the stake and reportedly said to the executioner: "You are going to burn a goose (Hus meant 'goose' in Czech) but soon you will have a swan you cannot roast or boil." This prophecy was later interpreted by Lutherans as foretelling Martin Luther's arrival a century later.

The nobility and people of Bohemia were enraged but did not take immediate action against the Church or German ethnic minority. Instead, they began to conduct their own services with their own clergy, separate from the German Catholic churches. They called themselves "Calixtines" from the Latin calyx, or in Slavic kalich, meaning chalice — an allusion to their practice of taking both the bread and the wine at communion.

In 1419 Wenceslas, King of Bohemia, died. Sigismund — the Holy Roman Emperor who had promised Hus safe passage through Germany, and brother to Wenceslas — claimed the Bohemian Crown for himself and swore to exterminate the heretics.

Jan Žižka

Mercenary · Royal Gatekeeper · Hussite Commander · President of the Bohemian Diet

Imitate your ancestors the ancient Bohemians, who were always able to defend the cause of God and their own. We are collecting troops from all parts, in order to fight against the enemies of truth and the destroyers of our nation and I beseech you to inform your preacher that he should exhort in his sermons the people to make war on the Antichrist and that everyone old and young should prepare himself for it. I also desire that when I shall be with you there should be no want of bread, beer, victuals, or provender and that you should provide yourselves with good arms. Remember your first encounter when you were few against many, unarmed against well-armed men. The hand of God has not been shortened. Have courage and be ready. May God strengthen you!

— Žižka of the Chalice, 1420

Jan Žižka first appears as the leader of a war band of Lapkas or mercenaries in the service of King Wenceslas of Bohemia. By 1410, after a royal pardon for "excesses" committed in the King's service, he was appointed Royal Gate Keeper. In 1410, Žižka was with the Bohemian force that aided the Polish-Lithuanian Union against the Teutonic Knights. He returned to Bohemia with an enhanced reputation and knowledge of the use of guns and war wagons on the battlefield.

Žižka joined Henry V of England against the French and participated in the battle of Agincourt on 25 October 1415. His participation in Henry's campaign prevented him from joining the other 452 Bohemian nobles in their protest to the Council of Constance. In 1419, after the death of King Wenceslas, Žižka took military control of the Hussites, specifically the Taborite faction.

Žižka's first action against those who opposed the Hussites was against a force raised by Queen Sophia, the widow of King Wenceslas. The Hussites were en route to a great field outside of Prague for an open-air religious service when mounted knights in Sophia's service set upon them. Žižka ordered the Hussite women to cover the ground between the Hussites and the knights with their gowns and head coverings. When the horses' feet were entangled in the clothing, their riders were thrown and the Hussites fell upon them and killed them to a man.

Žižka led the forces that defeated Sigismund's Crusade of 1420 and was elected President of the Bohemian Diet at Čáslav in 1421. Already blind in one eye, Žižka lost his other eye in the siege of Rabí in 1421, yet continued to lead his army. He halted Sigismund's Crusade of 1421, invaded Austria and Moravia in 1423, and fought against his fellow Hussites, defeating them at the Battle of Malešov. His lieutenant and later successor advised peace with the Utraquists when Žižka was en route to Prague to burn it to the ground. A peace was concluded and shortly thereafter Žižka died of the plague en route to invade Moravia.

Hussite Sects

Four Factions of the Movement

Utraquists

Moderate · Urban · Prague

The name derives from communion "sub utraque specie" — under both kinds. This was the moderate faction, comprised mostly of the merchant class and minor nobility in the urban areas, especially Prague. They benefited greatly from the confiscation of Catholic Church property. This faction attempted to negotiate with Emperor Sigismund in 1419–1420 without success.

Taborites

Radical · Egalitarian · Fortress of Tábor

The most radical sect, believing in equality under the law for noble and commoner, the equal distribution of wealth, communal ownership of property, and decision-making by elected council rather than noble fiat. Clergy were unnecessary, biblical interpretation was an individual matter, communion was held on hilltops, and children were baptized in rivers and ponds. They established themselves in the fortress town of Tábor.

Orebites

Disciplined · Military · Founded by Žižka

Established by Žižka in 1423 as the "Oreb Union," named after a hill near the town Orebovice. Dissatisfied with Taborite indiscipline, Žižka introduced strict military justice: all were equal under the law, loot was equally distributed, and hoarders were executed. Upon his death they took the name "Orphans" and carried a banner bearing Žižka's image on a white horse.

Adamites

Fringe · Short-lived · Suppressed 1421

A small, short-lived sect that believed all should live as Adam and Eve did. Their insistence on going naked doubtless contributed to their suppression by Žižka in 1421.

Ambush Under Flag of Truce

Žižka died of plague en route to invade Moravia, ending the career of one of the most remarkable military commanders of the medieval era. The Orebites renamed themselves "Orphans" in his honor.

Crusade of 1426

▾
15 June 1426
Battle of Ústí nad Labem
A Hussite force besieged Ústí nad Labem and were attacked by a much larger German Crusading army of 70,000 to 100,000 men (according to contemporary accounts). The Bohemian entrenched wagenberg of 500 wagons, established on high ground, defeated the Crusaders, who lost as many as 15,000 dead and 50,000 lost or wounded. The Hussites sustained 30 losses.

A Crusade led by Henry de Beaufort, Bishop of Winchester, was defeated by a Bohemian army comprised of Hussites and Catholics at Meiss. The Crusaders fled without a fight and suffered considerable casualties from the villagers they had pillaged en route.

Crusade of 1431

▾
14 August 1431
Battle of Domažlice
An army of 130,000 German Crusaders (according to contemporary accounts) led by Frederick of Brandenburg fled in panic at the sight of the Hussite force at Domažlice. The Crusaders suffered considerable casualties from the villagers they had pillaged en route.
1433

Compacta of Basle

▾
9 January 1433
Negotiations at Basle
Three hundred Hussites entered Basle and after 50 days of negotiation — on the verge of leaving — terms were reached granting them the Four Articles of Prague. The Articles remained the religious law of Bohemia until 1567, when the Hussites divided: some returned to Catholicism and some joined the Lutherans, drawing up the Confessio Bohemia, recognized by the Holy Roman Emperor in 1609 and granting considerable religious freedom to Bohemia. The violation of this agreement set the stage for the Thirty Years' War.
1434

Civil War of 1434

▾
Battle of Lipany
The Orebites and Taborites refused to accept the agreement of Basle. Their armies met a force raised by the nobility and Prague at Lipany and were defeated.
1457
George of Poděbrady Ascends
George of Poděbrady, Bohemia's only Hussite king, ascended to the throne and defeated a Hungarian Crusader army in 1468. After the death of Poděbrady, Bohemia became a part of the Holy Roman Empire.

The Polish-Lithuanian Alliance

Slavic Solidarity & Realpolitik

When the Polish-Lithuanian Union was under threat from the Teutonic Knights and their German and Hungarian allies, the Kingdom of Bohemia sent 3,000 soldiers — Jan Žižka among them — to aid their fellow Slavs. They were present at the battle of Grunwald in 1410 and participated in the siege of Malbork, the Teutonic Order's capital.

From 1421 to 1427 the Hussites received considerable military support from the Polish-Lithuanian Union, in the form of mercenaries, volunteers, and matériel. Jan Žižka and the Bohemian Diet went so far as to offer the crown to Ladislaus Jagiełło, King of Poland and a Lithuanian, who refused it under pressure from his Catholic clergy. The crown was then offered to Vytautas, the Grand Duke of Lithuania, who sent his nephew Zygmunt Korybut to serve as regent in Bohemia.

Korybut was unable, as he had hoped, to return the Bohemians to the Catholic Church and found himself at times fighting the Taborites as well as the Orebites. Official Polish-Lithuanian involvement came to an end in 1427 with the discovery of Korybut's plot to betray the Hussites to Emperor Sigismund. The Pope and Sigismund had engineered the Union's disengagement by threatening a crusade and providing support to the Teutonic Knights.

Despite Korybut's departure, it is safe to assume the continued presence of Poles and Lithuanians among the Bohemians. Once more in 1433, the Hussites joined the Polish-Lithuanian Union in a war against the Teutonic Knights and penetrated north all the way to the mouth of the Vistula River on the Baltic Sea.

The Hussite Armies

Organization, Doctrine & Innovation

They camped in the field with their women and children, who accompanied the army, as they had a large number of wagons with which they drew up a wall-like fortification. When they moved out for battle, they formed two lines of these wagons, which enclosed the foot troops, while the horsemen remained outside without moving off to any distance.

— Enea Silvio Piccolomini (later Pope Pius II), Historia Bohemia

The Hussite armies were predominantly peasant infantry armies with the minor nobility serving as light cavalry. There was no attempt to replicate the heavy armored knight so prevalent throughout the rest of Europe. The use of war wagons combined with crossbows and firearms can be attributed to Jan Žižka's experiences in Poland, but it was more than a technical innovation that led to so many successes against invaders and defenders alike.

The egalitarian nature of Hussitism resulted in a military force in which women as well as men were expected to fight, battlefield loot was evenly distributed, and all infractions were punished identically regardless of social status. Leaders were chosen based upon ability, not social standing. The Hussite army was well trained, disciplined, and ideologically motivated — unlike the vast majority of military forces of the time. All this, combined with the effective use of the most advanced technological innovations of the era, made a literally unbeatable combination.

Weapons & Tactics

The Hussite Arsenal

The War Wagon (Vozová hradba)

The war wagon is an ancient weapon with possible origins as old as the Scythians. The Saga of Haakon Broad Shoulder in Snorri Sturlason's Heimskringla claims the Norse Varangian Guard broke a wagenberg in Romania along the Danube around 1161.

Total per Wagon

Each wagon could be manned by 10–20 soldiers, men or women in keeping with Hussite practice. There were commanders for each wagon, per 10 wagons, and per 50–100 wagons, with one supreme commander for the infantry, wagons, and cavalry in the army.

Tactical Innovation

The Hussites did not lack for motivated manpower but were lacking in mounted forces. The war wagons arrayed in a linear or semi-linear formation — tied in with terrain impassable to horses, or in a laager on dominating terrain — could provide an active defense against a mounted or dismounted force of greater size.

An attacking formation was initially subject to concentrated crossbow and gunfire. If the attrited formation was still intact and advancing, they faced infantry with polearms fighting from inside or between the chained wagons of the wagenberg. Stones were even available for those in the wagon to throw at the attackers. At the critical moment when the attacking formation was in greatest disarray, the Hussite commander unleashed his typically small mounted force from within the wagenberg to attack and pursue. Once the pursuit began, the infantry came from behind the wagenberg and joined. It is a credit to the discipline of the Hussite mounted forces that there are no accounts of them pursuing a defeated enemy beyond the point of safety.

March Formation — Four-Column Order
When on the move, the wagons adopted a four-column formation. The baggage train moved in the center with two columns on either side.
Krajní (Outer Columns)

Longer than the inner columns, allowing them to form the complete outer circle of the wagenberg when the defensive position was established.

Placní (Inner Columns)

Shorter columns flanking the baggage train, forming the inner circle of the wagenberg in defensive configuration.

Crossbows

There were a considerable number of crossbows in the Hussite army, despite the tendency of historians to make far more of the presence of firearms. Crossbows shared with the firearms of the time the advantages of being easy to train and relatively uncomplicated to manufacture and maintain — all attributes making them ideal for armies comprised of large numbers of relatively inexperienced soldiers. The primary disadvantage was the time it took to prepare for firing.

Hand Gonnes (píšťala)

The handheld firearms of the time were essentially tubes on sticks ranging from .40–1.00 caliber. They could with practice be accurate out to a range of perhaps 50 yards. Given the smoke and noise when fired, they doubtless had greater unnerving effect on the enemy than their lethality.

Cannon

Tarasnice Light Artillery
[ Diagram: tarasnice ]

Like the hand gonne but of larger caliber, perhaps .75 to 1.00. Mounted on a small platform — initially without wheels, but wheels were added later. Employed between the wagons of the wagenberg.

Houfnice Heavy Artillery
[ Diagram: houfnice ]

The wheeled heavy artillery of the Hussites, employed on special wagons. There is reason to believe their first appearance was at the Battle of Ústí in 1426, though wheeled heavy artillery was already in use elsewhere in Europe. This piece would fire a projectile at least several inches in diameter.

Canister / Grape Shot

There is evidence supporting the use of canister, grapeshot, and buckshot in firearms against the Teutonic Knights in 1410, as well as by Hussite mercenaries fighting for the Poles in the late 15th century. Given this, it would be very probable that the Hussites used it as well.

Polearms

The Hussites used a variety of polearms, the primary purpose of which was to forcibly dismount a rider, making him more vulnerable to attack on foot. The weapon most commonly associated with the Hussites is the flail — an agricultural threshing tool with spikes placed on the short end, chained to a six-foot pole. Halberds, bardiches, axes, and any number of polearms found their way into Hussite hands as "battlefield pick-ups."

Armor & Swords

These would have come into the hands of most Hussites as a result of "battlefield pick-ups" and distributed loot.
